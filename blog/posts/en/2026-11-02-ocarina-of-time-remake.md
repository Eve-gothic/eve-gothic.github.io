There are games you remember, and there are games you carry with you like your grandmother's silver ring. *The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time* belongs to the second kind. On Thursday, November 5, it returns as a full remake, exclusively on Nintendo Switch 2. So I'm lighting a candle, putting the kettle on and telling you why this time even I — a lifelong remake sceptic — am waiting with colour in my pale cheeks.

## A quick history lesson

The original came out in 1998 on the Nintendo 64. It was the first fully 3D Zelda and the game that showed the industry how to fight and look around in three dimensions at all. Just think of Z-targeting, the enemy lock-on you'll now find in almost every action game. Metacritic still gives it 99/100. No wonder the remake is the centrepiece of the series' 40th anniversary.

The new version was announced in June, and Nintendo showed its first extended gameplay during a special Zelda presentation on September 8. Since then, my November to-do list has had exactly one item.

## What's changing

The list is concrete: completely overhauled visuals, fully orchestral music, voice acting in the story scenes and expanded dialogue. Link can finally jump and dash, the camera behaves like a modern game's, and you can aim ranged weapons with motion controls. There's also "Threads of Time", a feature that reminds you what has happened in the story and hints at where to go next. Perfect for anyone who — like me — can abandon a game for three weeks and come back with no idea why they're standing knee-deep in a lake.

My favourite additions? You don't have to play the ocarina with buttons: hum a melody or play it on a real instrument into the Switch 2 microphone, and Link's ocarina will respond. There's also the Hall of Fallen Heroes — a room of statues of defeated monsters, where you can read about them and challenge them to a rematch. A trophy gallery full of monsters? Sounds like my living room.

## The dark heart of Hyrule

The most interesting part, though, was Nintendo's "Ask the Developer" interview. Director HokWai Ng describes the principle the team followed: "the visuals may change, but the experience does not." That's why the world didn't become one seamless map like *Breath of the Wild* — just like in the original, the areas remain separate maps.

And art director Aya Shida says something I could get tattooed: Hyrule is beautiful, but it also "feels somewhat dark", while the characters who live there are colourful and quirky — and that contrast is what gives the game its mysterious, almost dreamlike charm. The team's early keywords were "a puppet-show feel" and "the analog feel of practical effects films". Somebody in Kyoto understands that the best fairy tale is one with a shadow waiting around the corner.

## Verdict before launch

I haven't played the remake yet, so I won't pretend to be a reviewer. But on paper everything adds up: respect for the original, an obsession with detail and one very gothic observation — a fairy tale without darkness is just a bedtime story. A few practical notes: the game takes about 27 GB, works in TV, tabletop and handheld modes, and Polish is among the supported languages. In 2027 two new amiibo figures will also arrive, unlocking special masks in the game.

On Thursday night I'm switching off the lights, leaving one candle burning and heading back to Kokiri Forest. If you hear someone humming Zelda's Lullaby from my room — it's not a ghost. It's me, testing the microphone.
