Ebenezer — Ti West's gothic Christmas, with Johnny Depp as Scrooge

On 13 November, Ti West's Ebenezer hits US theatres. Dickens, three spirits, Depp as Scrooge and McKellen as Marley — plus a tagline that feels like an invitation to a midnight screening.

I like Christmas in the dose Dickens never feared: fog, a long shadow on a cobbled street and someone knocking after midnight. So when Paramount announced Ebenezer, Ti West's film with Johnny Depp as Ebenezer Scrooge, I lit a candle faster than usual. The US theatrical release lands on Friday, 13 November 2026 — a date that belongs on a gothic novel cover, not in a catalogue of cosy holiday comedies.

The name you know, the story — according to the studio — you don't

The official tagline is exactly my kind of promise: "The name you know, the story you don't" — and right beside it, "Johnny Depp is Ebenezer Scrooge". This is not another polite, pastel stroll through London. At least in the marketing, it is a thrilling ghost story set in Dickens' London: one man on a supernatural journey through past, present and future, fighting for a second chance at redemption.

Nathaniel Halpern wrote the screenplay, based on Charles Dickens. Direction is Ti West's — a filmmaker who can build dread out of silence, a corridor and a door with something waiting behind it. Emma Watts is listed as producer. Distribution: Paramount Pictures. Runtime about 1 hour 56 minutes. In the US it carries a PG-13 rating for frightening material — so nobody is promising a cheerful tree without a shadow.

A cast like a wish list under a black Christmas tree

Depp as Scrooge is the obvious magnet, but the list does not stop there. Ian McKellen plays Jacob Marley — and that alone would make me buy a ticket before I even sat down. Alongside them: Rupert Grint, Andrea Riseborough, Sam Claflin, Daisy Ridley, Arthur Conti, Ellie Bamber, Henry Lloyd-Hughes, Charlie Murphy and Tramell Tillman. This is not "one star and a crowd". It is a cast that looks as if someone deliberately filled Dickensian London with people who can carry both drama and strangeness.

I mostly associate West with atmosphere: the sense that something is wrong before anything has happened. Dickens' A Christmas Carol was always a ghost story; decades of holiday branding just taught us to watch it through rose-coloured glasses. Here the studio sells a version where Scrooge's journey returns to that darker root.

Why this is exactly my kind of holiday

There is something deeply gothic in the Scrooge myth itself. A miser, chains, a door with the past standing behind it, and a future that does not ask whether you are ready. It is not horror for horror's sake — it is a morality play wrapped in a shroud.

Ebenezer also shows up in gothic season roundups — The Lace Ledger put it on the autumn slate precisely as the film where West takes Dickens and tells him through ghosts. I read that as an invitation: go back to the London of fog and cobbles, not the London of a shopping-mall advert.

I have not seen the film yet, so I will not pretend to be the reviewer at the end of the row. But on paper everything matches what I look for in November: a strong cast, a director who knows darkness, a literary foundation and a Friday-the-13th release. If someone is going to make me a gothic Christmas at the cinema, I would rather it be Ti West than another remake with warm cocoa in every frame.

Verdict before the screening

I have not bought tickets yet — I am waiting for the calendar to catch up with 13 November — but I already have the candles. Dickens' London, Depp as the man whose name we know too well, McKellen as Marley and the promise that we know the story less than we think. Sounds like an evening for my darkest dress and a phone left in my bag.

If you hear a quiet "bah, humbug" from my room — it is not a ghost. It is me, checking whether my voice is tuned for the screening.
